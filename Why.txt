Huh et al. (2024) — Platonic Representation Hypothesis. Different neural networks trained on different objectives converge toward similar representations. They show this for large vision and language models. They don't look at classical statistical models. They don't look at token-level attribution agreement. They don't operationalise it.

Zandieh et al. (2024) — TurboQuant. Random orthogonal rotation + polar coordinates + angle quantization for compression efficiency. They care about speed and memory. They don't use it as an attribution method. They don't ask what tokens survive quantization or whether the angular structure carries semantic signal.
Your PolarQuant — takes Zandieh's geometry and repurposes it as a third attribution vote. The tokens that survive angular quantization are the ones with geometrically stable representations. You then intersect those with attention tokens (A) and classical tokens (R) to get A∩R∩P — three structurally independent methods pointing at the same positions. That intersection is tighter than A∩R alone. That's your contribution, not Zandieh's.

Ruiz-Dolz et al. — Ramon's own work on argumentation scheme detection, argument mining at ACL/EMNLP. His framework identifies argumentative structure in text — claims, premises, relations. He doesn't do cross-method attribution. He doesn't look at whether different classifiers converge on the same tokens. But his concept of argumentative structure maps directly onto illocutionary intent — the parts of a sentence that carry persuasive force are exactly the parts your panel converges on.

Chen et al. (2026) — Attention Residuals. They replace fixed residual connections with learned attention over depth. They care about training dynamics and PreNorm dilution. They don't apply it to a heterogeneous panel. You took their depth-wise selection principle and applied it in two places: the backbone (Block AttnRes across layers) and the gate (AttnRes-style attention over heads). The god gate attending over the dumb and strong gates is a third level of the same idea — selective aggregation at every scale.

Aristotle — the illocutionary framing. Austin and Searle formalised speech acts in the 1960s, but the decomposition of utterances into locutionary (what is said), illocutionary (what is intended), and perlocutionary (what effect it has) goes back to Aristotle's Rhetoric. Your claim that cross-method convergence operationalises illocutionary force connects computational attribution to a 2,400-year-old framework. That's not something you need to prove computationally — it's the interpretive lens that makes the numbers meaningful. Ramon, as an argumentation researcher, will recognise this immediately.
The thread that connects all six: representations converge (PRH), and that convergence has geometric structure (TurboQuant/PolarQuant) that is stable across model families including classical statistics (your set-algebra), can be made selectively accessible across depth (AttnRes), and the tokens where convergence is strongest correspond to the positions carrying argumentative/illocutionary force (Ruiz-Dolz, Aristotle).
